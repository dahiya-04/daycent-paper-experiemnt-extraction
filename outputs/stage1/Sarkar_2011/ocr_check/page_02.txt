INTRODUCTION

Climate change and greenhouse gas emissions are the two crucial global
issues in the 21" century. The recent stagnation and decline in production of
the rice-wheat (Oryza sativa L. and Triticum aestivum L., respectively) crop-
ping system in India and other Southeast Asian countries are a result of both
climate change and reduced soil productivity. Hence, increasing productivity
and keeping pace with the rising food demand with minimum environmen-
tal disturbance have become a challenge to farmers and scientists. Decrease
in soil fertility is mainly due to use of inefficient management practices and
unsuitable ways of fertilizer application, especially N and overexploitation of
soil and water resources (Ladha et al. 2003). This situation can be overcome
very effectively by returning crop residues to the soil instead of burning
them and using proper and selected management practices for sustaining
the rice-wheat system, the production of which is proposed to increase by
supporting farmers and introducing national and regional policies to encour-
age food self-sufficiency (OECD-FAO 2007). Thus, this will help match the
growing food demand of South Asia and the world with the productivity of
the rice-wheat system in Indian subcontinent. Increase in productivity along
with efficient use of natural resources and minimal adverse impacts on the
rice-wheat growing environment can be achieved by sustainable agriculture,
however, sustainability of the rice-wheat systems in South and Southeast
Asian countries can only be achieved by selection of proper management
practices and adoption of new technologies.

The Lateritic and acid sandy loam soils of rice-wheat cultivated areas
of Eastern India are usually very low in organic carbon content. On this kind of
soil, farmers generally practice different types of intensive wet tillage, or pud-
dling, and dry tillage as essential management options for rice-wheat system
to conserve moisture and nutrient content in the profile (Sur, Prihar, & Jalota
1981; Cook, So, & Dalal 1992; Kushwaha, Tripathi, & Singh 2000, Tripathi,
Sharma, & Singh 2005). Both wet and dry intensive tillage practiced as con-
ventional management for the rice-wheat system in India were found to be
malefic for soil productivity, soil organic carbon content, and yield of the sys-
tem in the long term. Subsequently, reduced intensity and no-tillage have been
experimented with all around the world to reduce the negative impact on soil
