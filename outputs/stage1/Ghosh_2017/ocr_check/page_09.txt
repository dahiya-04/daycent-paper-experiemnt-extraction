Owing to natural soil variability in cultivated soil,
small changes in TOC is usually hard to assess. There-
fore, TOC is separated in different sub-pools, which are
recognized as more sensitive to crop and soil manage-
ment practices than TOC [17]. The role of labile
C-fraction in plant nutrient supply and crop productivity
has been established earlier [23]. In the study, active
C-pool constitutes the major share of upland soil; where
passive C-pool is the dominating C-pool in lowland.
This may be the results of different oxidation level
(particularly in rainy season) under contrasting ecosys-
tem upland and lowland system. Benbi et al. [23] like-
wise specified that puddle RW system facilitates greater
proportion (63%) of less-labile C-fraction (recalcitrant
C-fraction). In the same line, Majumder et al. [24]
reported that the less-labile and recalcitrant fractions
(fraction 3 and 4) together constituted about 55% of the
TOC in rice-based cropping systems in hot humid tropics
of India. Particular to lowland, the anaerobic condition
(during rice season) largely alters the composition of
TOC, which is dominated by less-labile Cfrac; and
recalcitrant pools [25]. Higher accumulation of resistant
compounds such as lignin has been reported in paddy
soils due to absence aerobic environments [26]. The
passive C-pool is of higher importance in subtropical
and tropical soils for its reduced oxidisability, less sen-
sitivity to crop and soil management, and long-term
persistence. Higher value of surface to subsurface active
C-pool ratio in pulse inclusive rotation indicates that
labile C enrichment is more prominent in surface soil.
This is possibly because of addition of more oxidisable
C through FYM and crop residues in the surface soil
layer. The results further demonstrate that in lowland
rice soil, a part of labile C moved to the sub-surface soil
that’s why lower values of surface to subsurface active
C-pool ratio were registered when compared with upland
system. Thus, the results are in favour of the postulated
hypothesis that inclusion of chickpea improves C-pools;
however it is only true for active C-pool.

Lability Index and Carbon Management Index

The inclusion of chickpea in RW system improved the LI
(14.1%) in surface soil; however, no significant improve-
ment was evident in upland. It is important to mention that
the mean LI value (1.70) was higher in upland than low-
land (1.37). Effect of nutrient management on LI was
prominent in both the production systems and found in the
order of organic > inorganic > control. The RWRC rota-
tion had higher CMI values over RW (Table 5). However,
the CMI values of MWMC and MW rotation was almost
comparable and difference was non-significant.

Lability index are being acknowledged as one of the
important parameters of soil quality [27]. Improved LI with
inclusion of chickpea supports the positive role of legume
on soil quality particularly on soil C. This increase in LI
might be attributed to higher easily oxidisable C-input
(crop residue and FYM) and belowground C-input (root C
and rhizo-deposition of C) in pulse inclusive rotation. The
higher LI values in organic and balance inorganic fertil-
ization are likewise indicating the role of C-input and
balanced nutrient management on soil C lability. “As
compared to a single measure of TOC, CMI can be used as
a more sensitive indicator of the rate of change of SOC in
response to cropping system and soil management change”
[4]. Addition of organic amendments increased the CMI in
both the production system. This is due to the increase in
annual C-input and the variations in organic matter quality,
thus modifying the lability of C to move to an oxidised
form [28]. In consistence with the present result, Vieira
et al. [29] also proposed that the legume crops are more
efficient compared to graminaceous species in improving
CMI values.

Carbon Input, C-Sequestration, C-Stabilization,
and Yield Advantage

The C-input through crop residue and FYM was estimated for
the last 8 years (2003-2011) under organic nutrient manage-
ment. Chickpea included system had higher C-input over RW
and MW rotations (Table 2). In lowland, the stabilization of
added C in the active C-pool was higher in RW over RWRC.
Conversely, in upland, despite higher annual C-input in
MWNMC the system efficiently stabilized higher active C-pool
than MW rotation (Fig. 3). Stabilization of added C in the
passive C-pool was higher under RW and MW rotations and
inclusion of chickpea reduced passive C-pool stabilization.
The increase in grain yield of rainy season crop (lowland rice
3.2-8.8%, upland maize 3.1—7.6%) was evident with inclu-
sion of chickpea (Fig. 4). The improvement in the yield of
rainy season cereal with the inclusion of chickpea was highest
under control plots and least in inorganic treatment, and the
trend was almost similar in both the production systems.
