Diversifying cereal-based systems with food legumes
(pulses) remains a conceivable option to improve the soil
quality. Owing to higher root biomass and leaf shedding
characters, pulses add a significant amount of C to soil [5].
Chickpea (Cicer arietinum L.)—a winter season pulse
crop, can be included in the cereal—cereal rotations for
system diversification. However, every year inclusion of
chickpea in place of winter cereal (wheat in particular) is
likely to have low farmer preference in assured irrigated
condition. Therefore, alternate-year rotation inclusion of
chickpea would be the more realistic approach for crop
diversification. Keeping these points in mind, here the
authors intend in evaluating the long-term impact of
alternate-year crop diversification in rice-wheat (lowland)
and maize—wheat (upland) rotations with legume compo-
nent (chickpea) on soil C-pools.

Comprehension of the complex process of C-seques-
tration and C-stabilization necessitates long-term experi-
ments [6]. Appraisal of the procedure is crucial in the
tropical climate where SOC content is naturally low and
improving SOC is a stiff challenge [7]. Fundamentally,
total organic carbon (TOC) consists of different C-frac-
tions. According to published literature some fraction
directly influences soil fertility and its quality [8]. Easily
oxidizable organic C (labile C-fraction) is more sensitive
indicators of agricultural management-induced changes
than total organic carbon (TOC) [9]. To have a better
appraisal of the mechanisms, total C stock is partitioned
into different fractions based on their oxidisability/lability
and persistence time [10] that can be used to assess long-
term changes in soil quality.

Here, the authors examined the long-term impact of crop
rotation and nutrient management on soil C-pool, C-man-
agement indices, C-stabilization, and C-sequestration from
two 8-year old (2003-2011) experiments (lowland rice-
based, upland maize-based). To date, the exploration of
data pertaining to the impact of pulse crop inclusion in the
cereal-based system on SOC has not been addressed ade-
quately [11]. Most studies in the field have only focused on
nutrient management only. The major hypotheses of the
study are (1) the alternate-year inclusion of chickpea in
cereal—cereal rotations increases the soil C-pools, (2). The
effect of crop diversification with legume component
(chickpea) on soil C-pools and C-stabilization differ in
contrasting production ecology i.e. lowland and upland
condition, (3). Improved SOC level with the inclusion of
chickpea improves the grain yield of cereal base-crop. The
findings of the study will surely advance our understanding
the mechanisms of C build-up or loss, characterization of C

Material and Methods
Soil and Site Characteristics

The data reported here were generated from two long-term
experiments carried out at main research farm of the
ICAR-Indian Institute of Pulses Research (ICAR-IIPR),
Kanpur, India (26°27’N, 80°14’E and 152.4 m above mean
sea level). The soils of study site belong to taxonomic class
Typic Ustochrept (Inceptisol) with sandy-loam texture. The
climate of the research site comes in sub-humid tropical,
receives an annual rainfall of 722 mm with the mean
annual maximum and minimum temperature is 33.0 and
20.0 °C, respectively. At the initiation of the experiment,
the SOC was 2.8 gkg~! soil in upland and lowland
experiment.

Experimental Design and Treatments

These two long-term experiments were initiated during the
rainy season (July) of 2003 with a major objective to
investigate the effect of different cropping systems and
nutrient management practices on soil health and crop
productivity. Both the experiments were laid in a split-plot
design with three replications; and the treatment comprised
of four cropping systems in the main-plot and three nutrient
managements in subplot in each year. In the present study,
the authors emphasized to unfold the impact of chickpea
inclusion in cereal—cereal systems. Therefore, the authors
selected alternate-year chickpea included rotation and
conventional cereal—cereal rotation from both production
system. Accordingly, the authors separated (1) maize—
wheat (MW), (2) maize—wheat—maize—chickpea (MWMC)
from upland; and (1) rice-wheat (RW), (2) rice—wheat—
rice—chickpea (RWRC) from lowland system. Both the
long-term experiments had common nutrient management
practices viz., (1) control (2) inorganic (NPKSZnB) and (3)
organic (all crop residues incorporation + biofertiliz-
ers + farmyard manure (FYM) with 5 Mgha~'). The
cultivar Azad Uttam (maize), PBW 343 (wheat), and DCP-
92-3 (chickpea) were used in the upland production sys-
tem; where, Pant Dhan 12 (rice), PBW 343 (wheat), and
KWR-108 (chickpea) were used in the lowland rice-based
experiment. Fertilizer nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P05) and
potassium (KO) (kg ha!) were applied in the form of
