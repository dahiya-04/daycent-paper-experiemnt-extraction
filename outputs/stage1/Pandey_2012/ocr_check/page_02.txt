for reduced economic investments with co-benefits of improved
fertility and carbon sequestration thereby offering significant CO

sions are influenced under reduced or no tillage practices are still
unclear. No tillage mainly brings about two modifications: (i) min-
imal soil disturbance and (ii) addition of plant residue in soil. These
two factors cause changes in soil properties and processes phys-
ically as well as biologically. Under absence of soil disturbance
moisture retention improves, aggregates are stabilized, organic
matter is protected and microbial communities are less disturbed
(Javurek and Vach, 2009; Soane et al., 2012). Absence of tillage
however, increases soil compactness which may in long term offer
problems in farming practices and hence tilling after certain gaps
may be needed (Soane et al., 2012). Increased resistance to gaseous
diffusivity under no tillage causes low mobility of gases along soil
profile thus affecting gaseous transport (Beare et al., 2009). No
tillage is often accompanied with harvesting of ripened ears while
leaving rest of the crop part in the field. Residues added are colo-
nized by decomposers thus producing simpler compounds which
act as substrates for products of which GHGs are a subset. These
processes ultimately guide production or consumption of GHGs,
relative proportions of the two leading to the observed net fluxes.
Under reducing conditions as is common in flooded rice fields,
residue incorporation tends to make soil more anaerobic since
organic compounds usually serve as electron donors (Mosier et al.,
2004). All these factors interact in complex ways thus influenc-
ing GHG emissions, transport and consumption. This may increase
emission rates of CH, (Alluvione et al., 2009), N20 (Ball et al., 1999;
Skiba et al., 2002; Regina and Alakukku, 2010) and CO2 (Oorts et al.,
2007; Almaraz et al., 2009).

Nitrification and denitrification, both contribute to NjO gener-
ation and hence good moisture along with extra N source added
through residues often increase N,O generation (Bateman and
Baggs, 2005). Some studies have also reported that no tillage acts as
a considerable sink for NO probably due to consumption in upper
layer (Grant et al., 2004; Almaraz et al., 2009).

The final products of microbial processes are, however, depen-
dant on the microbial communities and environmental conditions.
Complexities among these lead to high uncertainty and spatio-
temporal heterogeneity among the GHG source/sink estimates and
agricultural productivities among diverse croplands (Smith et al.,
2008). Hence more extensive monitoring comprising of different
cultivation practices, crops, regions and soil qualities is required
for adoption of better region specific management.

In north India, rice is cultivated during rainy season followed
by wheat in post rainy season, The rice-wheat system becomes
even more complex to generalize whether tillage or no tillage
will be beneficial from GHG management and agronomic yield
point of view. Therefore, possible tillage permutations are needed
to be studied so that beneficial selections can be made. With
this purpose, the present study was conducted in rice-wheat sys-
tem which is the predominant system in the entire Indo-Gangetic
plains. There were four tillage practices, i.e. tillage before every
crop sowing/transplanting, the conventional practice going on in
the region, tillage before sowing/transplanting of one crop (either
rice or wheat) and no tillage before propagation of other and no
tillage before sowing/transplantation of every crop or continuously
no tillage practice. As we did not come across any long term study
that incorporated tillage permutations, it was difficult to specu-
late how the emissions will be modified. Based on the literature
review, we expected that organic residue incorporation might lead
to higher CH, generation during rice cultivation as it reinforces
anaerobic conditions by serving as reducing agents besides pro-
viding organic substrates for methanogenesis. Addition of residues

is also expected to increase microbial respiration in aerobic zones
causing increased CO) emissions as well as increased N2O emis-
sions due to more availability of N. We hypothesized the following:

1. CHy emission rates will be the highest under continuous no
tillage and will be the lowest in continuously tilled soil.

2. N2O and CO, emission rates will be the highest in alternately
tilled soils due to residue incorporation but intermediate distur-
bance. In continuous no tillage practice, N)O and CO, emissions
will be more than in continuous no tillage.

3. Yield will remain unaffected under all permutations as nutrient,
water, pest or weed stress will be minimized following recom-
mended practices.

To check the hypothesis proposed, monitoring of all the three
GHGs was carried out over the rice growth and relevant soil proper-
ties and plant attributes were assessed. Objective was to compare
the fluxes under selected tillage permutations, how they vary along
the crop development and whether any relationships exist between
the assessed variables.

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Experimental site and tillage practices

The field experiment was conducted at the agricultural research
farm of the Institute of Agricultural Sciences, Banaras Hindu Univer-
sity, Varanasi, India where along term experiment on different crop
establishment methods in rice (cv PHB-71)—wheat (cv HD 2824)
sequence was conducted during 2003-2010 under all India Coor-
dinated Research Project on Integrated Farming Systems. The site is
located in eastern Indo-Gangetic plains at 25.14°N latitude, 82.03°E
longitude and 76.19 m above mean sea level. The experimental soil
(inceptisol) is sandy loam in texture. Soil properties on the date
of sowing/transplantation of rice in 2003 (at the start of tillage
experiment) and in 2009 are presented in Table 1. The experiment
employed four permutations of tillage and no tillage practices in
rice-wheat system. Under no tillage, harvesting of previous crop
was done manually leaving 20 cm straw standing. Minimal soil dis-
turbance took place under this practice. Leftover plant residues
decompose slowly with time. Seeds of the next crop were sown
between the standing residues with a zero till drill. In conventional
tillage practice, crops were harvested manually that removed crop
just above the soil leaving less than 5cm shoot along with roots,
which get mixed with the soil while ploughing the field for the next
crop. The treatments involved combination of two tillage practices
in rice as well as wheat. In rice, two tillage practices were trans-
planting of rice on puddle bed with repeated tillage accompanied
with final tilling and planking in standing water (RCT) and direct
sowing of rice using zero till drill (RNT). Whereas in wheat, the
two tillage practices comprised of conventional tillage with one
ploughing by disk plough and two harrowings at approximately
30cm depth by cultivator followed by planking (WCT) and zero
till drill sowing of wheat (WNT). The four treatments in the study
were: (i) conventional tilling and puddling before transplanting of
rice and conventional tilling before sowing of wheat (RCT-WCT),
(ii) conventional tilling and puddling before rice transplanting and
no tilling before sowing of wheat (RCT-WNT), (iii) no tilling before
rice sowing and conventional tilling before wheat sowing (RNT-
WCT), and (iv) no tilling before sowing of both rice and wheat
(RNT-WNT). Net size of each plot was 5.25 m x 5.25 mand the plots
were prepared in triplicates for each tillage permutation. In RNT-
WCT and RNT-WNT, during rice period, residue of previous wheat
crop was present, while in RCT-WNT and RCT-WCT, no consider-
